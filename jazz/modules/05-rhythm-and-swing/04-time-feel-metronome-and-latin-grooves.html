Time feel: metronome practice, bossa nova and jazz waltz

Why time feel is the skill that gets you hired

Ask any bandleader what they listen for in a new guitarist and the first answer is "time". Not speed, not vocabulary: whether the beat stays where it is when the guitarist plays, and whether playing with them feels easy. Good time is not a gift; it is the product of specific metronome work that most players never do because it is unglamorous. This lesson gives you that work, and then puts it to use in three feels where weak time shows immediately: the bossa nova, whose straight eighths expose any unconscious swing; the samba, which is fast and light; and the jazz waltz, where beat 1 is easy to lose.

The metronome tool has every setting used below: click on 2 and 4, click on beat 1 only, subdivisions, and dropped bars.

Subdivision: the foundation of steady time

Time wobbles when the space between beats is empty in your mind. Fill it. A player who feels eighth notes under every quarter note places the quarter notes better; one who feels sixteenths places them better still. The pyramid below moves from quarters up through eighths, triplets and sixteenths and back down, on one note. The test is simple: the beat must not move when the subdivision changes.

The subdivision pyramid
One note, click on 2 and 4. Each bar changes the subdivision; the beat must stay exactly where it was. Accent the first note of every beat lightly.

Three ways to practise it:

  • With the metronome's subdivision on, matching the bar you are in (eighths, triplets, none for the sixteenths). This checks your subdivisions are even.
  • With the subdivision off, click on 2 and 4. This checks that you can supply the subdivision yourself.
  • Silently. Count the subdivisions in your head while playing quarter notes only. This is what you will actually do on a gig.
Tip

Set the tempo where the sixteenth-note bar is just within reach. If every bar is easy, the exercise is not training anything. Typical starting point: 70–90 bpm.

Dropped bars: testing your internal clock

The metronome's drop bars setting silences the click for whole bars, every other bar or at random. Your job is to keep playing and to arrive exactly on the click when it returns. This simulates every real situation where the time reference disappears: a drummer who plays a fill, a bass player who stops walking for a bridge, a solo break, or an unaccompanied intro.

The exercise below is the simplest version: play two bars, then rest two bars while counting, then come back in. Use the Charleston so that each entry has a clear downbeat.

Play two, count two
Swing the eighths. Bars 3–4 are silent for you: count them aloud and come back in exactly on bar 1 of the next repeat. Then set the metronome to drop every other bar and keep the pattern going through the silence.

\tempo 100

\ts 4 4
(5.5 7.4 5.3 6.2).4{d ch "Dm7"} (5.5 7.4 5.3 6.2).8 r.2 | (5.5 5.4 4.3 6.2).4{d ch "G7"} (5.5 5.4 4.3 6.2).8 r.2 | r.1 | r.1 |

Progression of difficulty over the three weeks of this lesson:

  1. Click on 2 and 4, no dropped bars, 100 bpm.
  2. Drop every other bar.
  3. Drop randomly, one bar in three.
  4. Drop often, and lower the tempo to 70. At slow tempos a silent bar is nearly four seconds long; that is where you find out what your time is really like.
  5. Click on beat 1 only. One click per bar. If you can comp the F blues from the previous lesson for three choruses with one click per bar and never lose it, your time is reliable.

Slow practice

Slow practice is not for beginners; it is for everyone, because the slower the tempo the larger each error becomes. Two things happen at 60 bpm with the click on 2 and 4 that never happen at 160:

  • You hear exactly where within the beat you are placing each note, and you can choose to play right on it, slightly behind it (the relaxed "laid-back" feel) or slightly ahead (the "on top" feel that drives a band). All three are legitimate; what is not legitimate is not knowing which you are doing.
  • You hear the length of every note. Comping chords that are supposed to be short reveal themselves as sloppy or ringing.

Rule of thumb for any new material in this course: first clean pass at 60 bpm with the click on 2 and 4, then raise the tempo in steps of 4–6 bpm, each step only after three clean repetitions. It is slower than you want and faster than you expect.

Bossa nova comping

Bossa nova is Brazilian, from the late 1950s, and it is played with straight eighth notes. Nothing swings. The first problem for jazz players is unlearning the swing they have just worked so hard to build; the second is that the guitar plays two parts at once: a bass line with the thumb and chords with the fingers.

The pattern has two layers:

  • Bass (thumb): the root on beat 1 and the 5th on beat 3, each as a dotted quarter followed by an eighth that repeats the same note. Count "1 (2) and 3 (4) and". The thumb never stops; it is the surdo drum of a samba school reduced to one string.
  • Chords (fingers i, m, a on strings 4, 3 and 2): a two-bar rhythm related to the clave. Bar 1 attacks on 1, the and of 2, and 4 (the 3-3-2 cell from the previous lesson). Bar 2 attacks on 2 and 3. Then repeat.

The chord shapes are the top three notes of the drop-2 voicings you have been using; leave the bass string to the thumb.

Basic bossa nova over ii–V–I
Straight eighths, do not swing. Thumb on the lower staff voice: root on 1, 5th on 3, each repeated on the following 'and'. Fingers on the chords: bar 1 attacks on 1, and-of-2, 4; bar 2 on 2 and 3.

Practise the two layers separately for a week before combining them. Thumb alone, with the metronome's straight-eighths subdivision, until the dotted-quarter-plus-eighth bass is automatic. Then chords alone, counting the clave aloud. Then together, very slowly (60 bpm), and expect it to fall apart several times before it locks.

The full bossa bass line

Once the basic version is solid, learn the bass line that Brazilian guitarists actually play. The 5th is anticipated on the and of 2 and held through beat 3, and the root is anticipated on the and of 4 and held through beat 1 of the next bar. The pattern is written here on Cmaj7 for two bars; every chord uses the same rhythm with its own root and 5th.

The anticipated bossa bass line
Straight eighths. Thumb: root on 1 (held to the and of 2), 5th on the and of 2 held through 3 and 4, root again on the and of 4 tied into the next bar. The chord layer is unchanged.

Listen

João Gilberto's recordings from 1958–1961 are the source for the guitar pattern: listen to how quiet and even the thumb is and how little the chords move. For samba, listen to any Paulinho da Viola record for the guitar role, and to a samba-school percussion recording to hear the surdo accenting beat 2. The jazz-side reference is the Stan Getz and Charlie Byrd Jazz Samba album, where a jazz guitarist plays the Brazilian role.

Samba basics

Samba is the faster, older cousin of bossa nova, and it is felt in 2/4: two beats to the bar, with the heavy surdo drum accenting the second beat, not the first. That reversed accent is the single most important thing to get right. The guitar keeps the same thumb-and-fingers division of labour, but the chords move in sixteenth notes and the whole thing is lighter and quicker. The figure below is a simplified two-bar samba comping pattern; real samba guitarists vary it constantly, but this is the skeleton.

Basic samba in 2/4
Straight sixteenths at a brisk tempo. Thumb: root on 1, 5th on 2 with the accent on 2 (the surdo). Fingers: bar 1 attacks on 1, the 'a' of 1 and the 'and' of 2; bar 2 on the 'e' of 1, beat 2 and the 'and' of 2.

Count sixteenths as "1 e and a 2 e and a". Set the metronome to 2 beats per bar and start at 80 bpm; samba sits at roughly 100–120 bpm in 2/4 (which feels like 200–240 if you count it in 4). The chords must be very short and quiet; the thumb and the accent on 2 are what people hear.

The jazz waltz

Jazz in 3/4 came into the repertoire in the 1950s and 1960s and stays there. Standard 3/4 comping avoids the "oom-pah-pah" of a Viennese waltz: beat 1 is often left empty or anticipated, and the most common figure is a dotted-quarter pair, a chord on 1 and a chord on the and of 2, which is the Charleston squeezed into three beats. Swing the eighths as in 4/4.

The waltz below runs twice through a ii–V–I in F and uses a different figure in nearly every bar: dotted-quarter pair; 1 and the and of 2 held; 2 and 3; 1 and a short 3; the and of 1 held then 3; a push on the and of 3 into the I chord, held for a whole bar; and short hits on 2 and 3. Count every bar as "1 and 2 and 3 and" before playing.

Jazz waltz comping over ii–V–I in F
3/4, swing the eighths, click on beat 2 only or on all three beats with beat 1 accented. Each bar is a different figure; name it before you play it.

Watch out

In 3/4 the pushes and empty downbeats make it easy to lose beat 1, and losing beat 1 in a waltz is far more audible than in 4/4. Count aloud for the first week, tap your foot on 1 only, and use the metronome with 3 beats per bar and the click on beat 1 only as the final test.

Exercises

  1. Subdivision pyramid at 70, 85 and 100 bpm, each of the three ways (subdivision on, off, silent). Five minutes.
  2. Play two, count two, then dropped bars every other bar, then random, at 100 and at 70 bpm.
  3. Bossa thumb alone for a week at 100 bpm with the straight-eighths subdivision on; then chords alone; then together at 60, 80, 100, 120.
  4. Full bossa bass on Cmaj7, then on the whole ii–V–I, at 90 bpm.
  5. Samba at 80 bpm in 2/4, thumb accenting beat 2; raise to 100 over the three weeks.
  6. Jazz waltz at 120, then 140, then 170, click on beat 1 only for the last pass.
Practice

Daily, 15 minutes:

  • 3 min: subdivision pyramid at 80 bpm, subdivision off, click on 2 and 4.
  • 3 min: the F blues from the comping lesson with dropped bars at 100 bpm (week 1: every other bar; week 2: random; week 3: click on beat 1 only).
  • 5 min: bossa nova. Week 1 thumb and chords separately; week 2 together at 60–100; week 3 the full anticipated bass line, 100–120 bpm, over Dm7–G7–Cmaj7–Cmaj7 and then over Gm7–C7–Fmaj7–Fmaj7.
  • 2 min: samba at 80–100 bpm in 2/4.
  • 2 min: jazz waltz at 140, a different figure every bar.

Record one bossa chorus and one waltz chorus at the end of week 3 and listen for two things only: are the eighths really straight in the bossa, and does beat 1 ever move in the waltz?
