Your instrument, tone and technique for jazz

Set up any guitar for a warm jazz tone, learn how to sit, hold the pick and place both hands so that fast tempos stay relaxed, and start the daily chromatic exercise with a metronome.

Why start here

Most of what makes a jazz guitarist sound like a jazz guitarist is not harmony. It is a warm, round tone, clean articulation and a relaxed technique that survives a fast tempo for forty choruses. Players who skip this lesson spend years fighting a thin tone, a tense left hand and a pick that flies out of their fingers at 240 bpm. Thirty minutes of setup and a ten-minute daily exercise will save you all of that. Nothing in this lesson needs theory; it needs your ears and a little patience.

Any guitar works

The classic jazz sound comes from a hollow archtop with a neck pickup, but you do not need one. Every type works if you set it up for the job:

  • Archtop (full hollow): the warmest, most acoustic tone and the most feedback at stage volume. The reference sound of the 1940s–60s.
  • Semi-hollow: a centre block tames the feedback; a slightly tighter, more focused tone. The most versatile choice for a working player.
  • Solid-body: completely usable. Many great modern jazz records are made on solid-bodies. Use the neck pickup, roll the tone down a little and nobody will complain.
  • Nylon-string classical / steel-string acoustic: fine for learning everything in this course. Chord-melody and Brazilian styles sound superb on nylon.

What matters more than the body is the pickup you use, the strings, and how you play. Do not postpone practising until you own the "right" instrument.

Strings and gauge

Two choices shape your tone more than any amp knob:

  • Flatwound vs roundwound. Flatwound strings have a smooth, polished wrap. They sound darker, with less sustain and almost no finger squeak; this is the traditional jazz sound. Roundwounds are brighter, with more sustain and more high-end "zing". Both are legitimate. If you want the classic sound quickly, fit flatwounds. If you also play other styles on the same guitar, roundwounds with the tone knob rolled off get you most of the way.
  • Gauge. Jazz players typically use heavier strings than rock players: a set starting at 0.011 or 0.012 on the high E string is common. Heavier strings give a fuller fundamental and a more even response across the neck, and they stay in tune when you dig in with a thick pick. If you come from 0.009 or 0.010 sets, move up one gauge at a time and have the guitar set up (truss rod, nut slots, intonation) when you change gauge.

Whatever you choose, keep the strings in reasonable condition. Dead flatwounds can last a year or more; dead roundwounds sound dull and intonate badly.

Pickup, tone control and amp

Start from these settings and adjust by ear:

  • Neck pickup. The neck pickup picks up the string where it moves most, giving the roundest tone. Use the bridge pickup for brighter Latin or fusion sounds only when you want them.
  • Guitar tone knob at roughly 5–7 out of 10. This removes the harsh top end without making the sound muddy. On a bright solid-body, go lower. You should still hear the attack of the pick clearly.
  • Guitar volume at 8–10. Leave headroom to turn down for comping behind a soloist.
  • Amp: clean, with headroom. Treble around 3–4, middle around 5–6, bass around 4–5, no overdrive, a touch of reverb at most. Jazz tone is a clean tone; if the amp starts to break up when you play a chord, turn the master up and the gain down. Solid-state and modelling amps are fine; the "jazz combo" sound comes mostly from a flat, clean response and a 10- or 12-inch speaker.
Tip

Play a single note on string 3 around the 5th fret, then play it again with the tone knob fully open. If the second note sounds "fizzy" at the top, you had it right the first time. Then play a four-note chord: if the low notes blur together, cut the bass on the amp, not the tone knob.

Pick, fingers or both

All three are used by professional jazz guitarists. The course notates everything so that any of them works; choose by sound and by what you want to play.

  • Pick (plectrum). The standard for single-note bebop lines and the most reliable at fast tempos. Use a thick pick (about 1.0–1.5 mm, or a small "jazz" pick). A thin pick bends and produces a click; a thick one lets the string do the work. Hold it between the pad of the thumb and the side of the index finger, with only 2–3 mm of the tip showing. Grip firmly enough that it does not rotate, and no firmer.
  • Fingers. Thumb and three fingers (as in classical guitar, or with a soft thumb-side attack) give a warm, piano-like sound for chord melody and can play any voicing without worrying about muted inner strings. Many solo-guitar players use fingers only.
  • Hybrid. Pick held normally, middle and ring fingers pluck the upper strings. This gives you single lines with the pick and clean chord "pads" with the fingers, and it is the default for many modern players. If you already pick, add the fingers gradually; the fingerstyle lesson in the chord-melody module covers it in detail.

The exercises below say "pick"; if you play with fingers, alternate index and middle finger (i–m) where the text says "down–up".

Four strokes on the same note, moving the pick from over the neck pickup towards the bridge. The last two are louder and brighter; the first two are the jazz sound.

Where you strike the string is a tone control: over the end of the fretboard or the neck pickup is dark and round; close to the bridge is bright and hard. The default jazz position is roughly over the neck pickup. Move towards the bridge for accents and for Latin comping.

Posture

Technique problems usually start with how the guitar sits. The rules are the same seated or standing:

  1. Neck angled upward, around 20–30 degrees, headstock roughly at shoulder height. A horizontal neck forces the left wrist to bend sharply.
  2. Guitar body against your torso, not sliding forward on your thigh. Seated, rest the waist of the guitar on your right thigh (or left thigh with a footstool, classical style); either is fine as long as the neck angle stays up.
  3. Use a strap even when sitting, adjusted so the guitar is in the same place when you stand. Then nothing changes on the gig.
  4. Both shoulders down, back straight but not rigid. Every ten minutes, drop your arms and shake out your hands. Tension is cumulative and you will not notice it until it hurts.

The left hand: economy

Jazz lines need the left hand to move very little. Three habits give you that economy:

  • One finger per fret. With the index finger at a given fret, the middle, ring and little fingers each own the next fret up. Four frets, four fingers, no sliding around. This is what "playing in position" means; the scale lesson builds everything on it.
  • Thumb behind the neck, roughly opposite the middle finger, pointing up or slightly toward the headstock. This lets all four fingers reach their frets with curved joints. Wrapping the thumb over the top is useful for a few chord shapes later, but as a default it kills the little finger.
  • Fret just behind the fret wire, with the minimum pressure. Press a note, then slowly release until it buzzes; the correct pressure is just above that. Keep the unused fingers within a centimetre of the strings, hovering over their frets, rather than flying away.

Fingers are numbered 1 (index) to 4 (little finger); in the notation they appear as small numbers beside the notes. The thumb is 5 on the rare occasions it is used.

The right hand: alternate picking

For single notes, move the pick down on the beat and up on the "and". This is alternate picking and it is the default for everything in the course until Module 5 discusses other options. The motion comes from the wrist (with a little forearm rotation), not from the elbow and not from the thumb joint. Keep the motion small: the pick should travel only a few millimetres past the string. Rest the side of the palm or the little finger lightly on the bridge or the guitar top for a reference point; do not clamp it.

When you cross to a new string, keep the alternation going (down–up–down–up) regardless of the string change. This is harder than it looks and it is exactly what the chromatic exercise trains.

The chromatic exercise

This is the one daily exercise of this lesson. It trains one-finger-per-fret, minimal pressure, alternate picking across string changes, and metronome discipline all at once.

Place finger 1 at fret 1 (position I). On each string play frets 1, 2, 3, 4 with fingers 1, 2, 3, 4. Keep each finger down until the next string: when finger 4 plays fret 4, fingers 1, 2 and 3 should still be holding frets 1, 2 and 3. Then lift all four and start on the next string.

Fingers 1-2-3-4, one per fret. Pick down-up-down-up throughout. Leave each finger down until you change string.

Coming back down, reverse the finger order on each string (4-3-2-1). Note that the picking does not reverse: the pick still goes down on the beat.

Three refinements once the basic version is clean:

  1. Move the position. Play the same thing with finger 1 at fret 5, then fret 9. Higher positions have narrower frets and are easier on the stretch; position I is the hardest, which is why we start there.
  2. Permute the fingers. Play 1-3-2-4, then 2-4-1-3, then 4-2-3-1 on every string. These break the habit of only ever playing fingers in order.
  3. Change the subdivision without changing the tempo. Quarter notes, then eighth notes, then triplets, then sixteenths at the same metronome setting. The rhythm lesson explains these values; for now: one note per click, two per click, three per click, four per click.

Watch out

A stretch should feel like work, never like a sharp or burning pain in the wrist, forearm or the base of the thumb. If it does, stop, shake out, check that the thumb is behind the neck and the neck is angled up, and come back ten minutes later at a slower tempo. The chromatic exercise in position I is a stretch for most hands; start at position V if yours is small.

Using the metronome from day one

Set the metronome to 60 bpm and play the ascending exercise with one note per click. The goal is not speed; it is that every note lands exactly with the click, every note sounds the same volume, and the hand stays relaxed. Only when a tempo is clean for three consecutive repetitions do you raise it, and then by no more than 4–8 bpm. Most students reach 120 bpm in eighth notes (240 notes per minute) within a few months; speed is a by-product of relaxation and accuracy, never the other way round.

A small but important habit: when a repetition goes wrong, do not stop the metronome. Keep it running, drop back in on the next bar, and finish the repetition. Jazz is played with a rhythm section that does not stop either.

Checklist for your first week

  • Guitar set up: neck pickup selected, tone knob at roughly 5–7, amp clean with the treble low.
  • Strings: a jazz-friendly gauge (0.011 or heavier) in good condition; flatwounds if you want the classic tone.
  • A thick pick, or a plan for fingers/hybrid.
  • Strap adjusted so the neck angles up and the guitar is in the same place seated and standing.
  • Thumb behind the neck, one finger per fret, minimum pressure.
  • Alternate picking, small motion from the wrist.
  • Ten minutes of the chromatic exercise with the metronome every day.
Practice
  1. Chromatic exercise, position I, ascending and descending, quarter notes at 60 bpm. Three clean repetitions, then raise the tempo by 4 bpm. Four minutes.
  2. Same exercise in eighth notes at the same tempo (two notes per click). Three minutes. Listen for even volume across every string change.
  3. One finger permutation (1-3-2-4 this week, 2-4-1-3 next week) in position V, quarter notes. Three minutes.
  4. Record 30 seconds of the exercise on your phone once a week. Listen for notes that are louder or shorter than their neighbours; those mark the string crossings or fingers you need to slow down for. Write your tempo in a practice log; the practice plans page has a template.
