Lifelong practice and musician's health

Design practice that keeps improving through plateaus for decades, protect your hands, back and hearing, manage performance anxiety, use mental practice, and set a continuing study plan beyond this course.

Why the long game is the only game

Everything in this course assumes you will still be playing at seventy. Most of what stops musicians is not lack of talent; it is injury, hearing loss, burnout, stage fright that never got addressed, and practice that stopped producing results and was quietly abandoned. All five are preventable with habits that cost minutes a day. This lesson is about those habits. It gives general guidance that experienced players and teachers agree on; it is not medical advice. If something hurts, if your hearing changes, or if anxiety is affecting your life, see a doctor, an audiologist or a therapist who works with performers; they exist, and they are good.

Practice design

What practice is for

Practice is the deliberate work of making something you cannot do into something you can, and then into something you do not have to think about. It is not playing through tunes you know (that is maintenance, also necessary), and it is not noodling (that is pleasure, also necessary). A professional's session has all three, in known proportions, and the practice part is where the improvement happens. The practice plans give the structure; this section gives the principles that make the structure work for decades.

  • One clearly defined problem per slot. "Play the drop-2 inversions of m7b5 on strings 5-4-3-2 through the cycle at 100 bpm without hesitation" is a practice item. "Work on voicings" is not.
  • A measurable target, usually a tempo, a number of keys, or a number of error-free repetitions. Log it. The log is the only reliable evidence that practice is working, because day-to-day feeling is noise.
  • Slow enough to be correct. Every repetition at a tempo where you make mistakes is a rehearsal of the mistake. Find the tempo where it is right, and raise it in small steps.
  • Short, frequent, focused. Four twenty-minute blocks with breaks beat one ninety-minute slog. Attention, not hours, is the limiting resource.
  • Vary the context. A phrase learned in one key, one position and one tempo is known in one place. Move it: twelve keys, three positions, with the metronome on 2 and 4, over a tune, at double time, played as a question and answered. Motifs and phrasing and Shells through all keys are models of this.
  • Interleave. Alternate items within a session (voicings, then reading, then back to voicings) rather than blocking them; it feels less productive and produces better retention.
  • End by recording one chorus and listening, as in Tone, recording and live sound.

Plateaus

Every player plateaus, repeatedly, for weeks or months. A plateau is not a lack of talent; it is a sign that the current practice has extracted what it can. Four responses work:

  1. Change the measure. If tempo has stalled, measure accuracy, keys, or consistency across takes instead. Progress often moves to a dimension you were not watching.
  2. Change the input. Transcribe a different player, learn a tune in an unfamiliar style, study a different instrument's vocabulary, take lessons from someone new. Plateaus are often starvation.
  3. Go back to fundamentals at a higher standard. Re-do the chromatic exercise from lesson 1.1 with a stricter ear for evenness, re-do guide tones with better voice leading. The basics at a professional standard are a different thing from the basics at a beginner's.
  4. Rest. A week without the instrument, after months of work, often ends a plateau by itself. Consolidation happens while you are not practising.

Keep a list of plateaus you have broken, with what broke them. The next one will be less frightening.

Injury prevention

Guitarists are prone to repetitive-strain problems of the hands, wrists and forearms, to shoulder and neck pain, and to back pain from sitting badly for hours. Nearly all of it is caused by tension, poor posture, sudden increases in practice load, and playing through pain. The prevention is well known:

  • Posture. Sit or stand so that the spine is long, the shoulders are down and the neck is not craned to see the fretboard. A strap, even when seated, keeps the guitar at the same height every time and stops you hunching. Use a footstool or a support only if it keeps your back straight. Change position every twenty minutes.
  • Hand economy. Light pressure (the minimum that makes the note clean, which is far less than most players use), the thumb behind the neck and relaxed, the wrist as straight as the chord allows, no stretches beyond what the hand does comfortably. Big stretches are a request, not a requirement: most voicings have a stretch-free alternative, and the fourth finger exists.
  • Warm up. Five minutes of slow, easy, stretch-free playing before anything demanding, especially in cold rooms and before gigs. The example below is a warm-up designed for exactly this: one position, one finger per fret, no stretches, light touch, played slowly with attention to the feel of each finger landing and releasing.
  • Load management. Increase practice time gradually. A player who goes from one hour a day to five hours the week before an exam is the player who gets injured. The same applies to a sudden run of gigs and to new, physically different material (a chord-melody programme after years of single lines).
  • Breaks. Five minutes every half hour. Put the guitar down, stand, shake the arms out, look at something far away.
  • Stop when it hurts. Discomfort from new work fades in minutes; pain that persists, tingles, numbness, weakness or loss of control are signals to stop, rest and, if they recur, see a doctor or a physiotherapist who works with musicians. Playing through it turns weeks into months.
  • General condition. Sleep, hydration, basic fitness and a warm pair of hands matter more than any exercise.

One finger per fret, 1st finger at fret 5, hand still. Press as lightly as possible; release each finger as the next lands. Slow, even, no accent. Repeat three times, then move the whole pattern up two frets and repeat.

Hearing protection

Hearing damage is cumulative, permanent, and common among musicians, including jazz musicians, who assume it only happens to rock drummers. A ride cymbal a metre from your ear, a loud horn section, a monitor wedge, headphones for hours while transcribing: all of these are enough over years. The rules:

  • Wear musician's earplugs on any loud gig and at every rehearsal with a drummer. Flat-response plugs (the kind with a filter that lowers the volume evenly) are inexpensive; custom-moulded ones are better and last for years. They take a week to get used to and then you will not play without them.
  • Keep headphone and monitor levels down. When transcribing, set the level once, low, and resist raising it; take the headphones off for five minutes every half hour. In-ear monitors on stage can be safer than wedges if the level is sensible; they can also be the loudest thing you own.
  • Position yourself away from the drummer's cymbals and horn bells where the stage allows, and never with a monitor pointing at your ear from close range.
  • Watch for the warning signs: ringing after a gig, muffled hearing the next morning, difficulty following conversation in a noisy room. Ringing that does not fade or persistent change in hearing is a reason to see an audiologist promptly. Get a baseline hearing test now, and repeat it every few years; it is quick and it is what you will wish you had done.

Protecting your hearing is not caution; it is protecting the instrument you cannot replace.

Performance anxiety

Almost every performer experiences anxiety before and during performance; the professionals are the ones who have learned to work with it. The useful facts:

  • The physical symptoms (fast heart, shaking hands, dry mouth, cold fingers, racing thoughts) are the body's normal arousal response, not evidence that something is wrong. Labelling them as excitement rather than fear changes how they affect your playing more than trying to suppress them.
  • Preparation is the main treatment. Most stage fright is well-founded: the material is not secure. Over-learn it: play the hard tune slower and faster than needed, in the dark, standing, after running up the stairs, with someone watching. The feeling of "I could do this in my sleep" is what you carry on stage.
  • Simulate performance in practice: record "one take only", play for friends, play at sessions, play through mistakes without stopping, as in Sight-reading. Each exposure lowers the next one.
  • Breathe. Slow exhalations before walking on (a longer out-breath than in-breath, a few times) measurably lower arousal. Do it in the wings, in the toilet, behind the amp.
  • Have a pre-performance routine that is the same every time: warm up, tune, check the set list, a few breaths, a specific thought about the first tune. Routine replaces rumination.
  • Direct attention outward: to the sound of the band, the bass line, the drummer's ride, the lyric. Anxiety lives in attention turned inward to your own hands and your own judgement.
  • Avoid self-medication. Alcohol and other substances degrade playing and make the next gig's anxiety worse. If anxiety is severe, persistent or stopping you from working, a doctor or a psychologist who works with performers can help, with approaches that have good evidence; there is no professional stigma in getting that help, and many of the best players have.

Mental practice

Mental practice (practising without the instrument: hearing the music in your head, visualising the fretboard, feeling the movements) is used by every professional, often without noticing. Done deliberately it is one of the most efficient tools you have, because it is available on trains, in waiting rooms and in bed, and because it builds exactly the internal map that fast playing depends on.

  • Visualise the fretboard: name the notes of a chord, see the shape, see the next inversion, without a guitar. Use the fretboard trainer to check.
  • Hear before you play: sing a phrase, then imagine playing it (where the fingers go), then play it. Over time the gap between hearing and playing closes; that is what "playing what you hear" means.
  • Rehearse a performance: the set list, tune by tune, with the intros, the endings, the cues, the difficult passage played perfectly in your mind, the feeling of calm in the first bar.
  • Analyse tunes away from the instrument: write the numerals of a standard from memory, work out the key centres, plan voicings. The ear trainer on the commute counts as mental practice too.
  • Review: after a gig or session, replay the good parts and the mistakes mentally, decide what to practise, and let it go.

Ten minutes of concentrated mental practice is worth more than thirty minutes of distracted playing. The two combine: play a passage slowly, then play it in your head, then play it again.

A continuing study plan

This course ends here; study does not. A professional's continuing education looks like this, year after year:

Area Ongoing commitment
Repertoire A list of tunes you can call, growing by one or two a month, reviewed on a rota; the repertoire page is the starting list
Vocabulary A transcription in progress at all times; phrases moved through twelve keys and into tunes
Harmony A reharmonisation or chord-melody arrangement in progress; one new voicing concept a month, through the cycle
Reading Fifteen minutes a day, forever, as in Sight-reading
Technique A short daily maintenance routine (the warm-up above, the chromatic exercise, arpeggios in position) at a logged tempo
Ear Daily minutes in the ear trainer; singing everything you play
Listening An album a week, listened to properly, across eras and instruments; the list on the repertoire page is a decade of work
Study A teacher, a masterclass, a book or a course each year that is outside your comfort zone: a different instrument's method, a different style, composition, arranging, production
Recording A dated folder of your playing, reviewed monthly
Health Earplugs in the case, breaks in the session, a hearing test on the calendar, and the honesty to stop when something hurts

Treat the plan as a cycle, not a ladder. The material in Modules 1–14 is not finished when you can play it; it is finished when you can teach it, and even then the standard rises. Players who are still improving at sixty are the ones who kept the basics on the daily list and never stopped transcribing.

Exercises

  1. Audit your practice. For a week, log every session with slots, targets and results. At the end, mark each slot as practice, maintenance or noodling, and rebalance.
  2. Plateau list. Write down your current plateau (there is always one), then try each of the four responses for a week each and note which moved it.
  3. Posture and pressure check. Film yourself playing for five minutes from the side; look at the neck, shoulders and wrists. Then play a chord progression with progressively less left-hand pressure until notes buzz, and back off one notch: that is your working pressure.
  4. Hearing baseline. Book a hearing test. Buy musicians' earplugs and use them at the next loud rehearsal.
  5. Mental run-through. Before your next gig, rehearse the whole set in your head once, with intros, endings and cues; afterwards, note which tunes went as imagined.
Practice

Every session, from today. The warm-up above (3 minutes, light pressure), a five-minute break every thirty minutes, the cool-down at the end (1 minute), one chorus recorded, one line in the log. Earplugs at every rehearsal with a drummer.

Daily, 10 minutes away from the guitar. Mental practice: visualise one voicing set through the cycle, sing one phrase and imagine playing it, write the numerals of one tune from memory.

This month. Book a hearing test; film your posture; write your continuing study plan for the next twelve months using the table above, with one item per row and a date for each; show it to a teacher or a colleague.

Every year. Re-read this lesson, re-do the audit, update the plan.
