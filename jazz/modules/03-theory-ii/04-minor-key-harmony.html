Why minor needs its own lesson

A major key has one scale and seven chords. A minor key has three scales, and jazz borrows chords from all of them at once. That is not a complication to be endured; it is what makes minor-key tunes sound the way they do. The ii chord is half-diminished, the V chord carries extra tension (a flat 9th, often a flat 13th), and the tonic chord can be a m6, a mMaj7 or a m7 depending on the bar. A large share of the repertoire is in minor or passes through minor keys, and the minor ii–V–i is the second most common progression after its major cousin. You met the three minor scales in The three minor scales; now you will harmonise them.

Natural minor

The first table is simply the relative major renumbered (lesson 2). Its weakness is on degree 5: a minor seventh chord with no tritone, so no pull home. Natural minor also gives us two chords that jazz loves: bVImaj7 and bVII7, the "backdoor" sounds that you will study in Module 11.

Harmonic minor

Raise the 7th degree of the scale by a half step and the chord on degree 5 becomes a true V7, with the tritone and the pull. The tonic becomes i(maj7), a minor-major seventh, and the chord on degree 7 becomes a fully diminished seventh (vii°7). The ii chord is still half-diminished (iiø7), because degrees 2, 4, 6 and 1 of harmonic minor are the same as in natural minor. This is the scale that gives minor keys their cadence; almost every minor ii–V–i uses the V7 from harmonic minor.

Melodic minor

Raise the 6th as well and you get melodic minor, the "jazz minor". Its tonic chord is again i(maj7), and because the 6th degree is now major, a i6 chord (m6) is also available: that is why m6 and mMaj7 are the two standard tonic sounds in minor. Melodic minor also turns the ii chord into a plain ii7 and the IV chord into a dominant IV7, two sounds you will meet in Module 9 when you study the melodic-minor modes. For now, the thing to take from it is the pair of tonic chords.

Theory note

From natural minor: iiø7, iv7, bVImaj7, bVII7 (and i7 in modal or Dorian-flavoured tunes). From harmonic minor: V7 with a b9 (and b13), i(maj7). From melodic minor: i6 and i(maj7). The composite "jazz minor key" is therefore: i6 / i(maj7) – iiø7 – bIIImaj7 – iv7 – V7b9 – bVImaj7 – bVII7. A chart in a minor key will mix these freely; none of them is a surprise.

The minor ii–V–i

Take degrees 2, 5 and 1 from the harmonic-minor table and you have the minor cadence: iiø7 – V7 – i. In A minor the chords are below; the engine shows the notes.

Compare this with the major ii–V–I in lesson 3. The ii chord's 5th has dropped a half step (half-diminished instead of minor). The V7 is the same quality as in major. The tonic is minor, with a major 7th or a 6th. The root motion (down a 5th, down a 5th) and the guide-tone logic are identical to the major version: the 7th of iiø7 falls a half step to the 3rd of V7, the 3rd of iiø7 stays as the 7th of V7, the 7th of V7 falls a half step to the 3rd of i, and the 3rd of V7 rises a half step to the root of i (or stays as the 7th of i(maj7)).

Where the b9 and b13 come from

On a chart the dominant of a minor key is usually written V7b9 (or V7b9b13, or V7alt, or simply V7 with the tensions understood). The b9 and b13 are not added from outside: they are the two notes of the harmonic minor scale that are not already in the plain V7 chord. In A harmonic minor, look at the table: the scale's 6th degree (the root of the bVImaj7 chord) sits a half step above the root of E7, which makes it the b9 of E7. The scale's 3rd degree (the root of the bIII chord) sits a half step above the 5th of E7, which makes it the b13. Build E7 with those two notes and you get the full minor-key dominant:

SymbolNameFormulaNotes
E7dominant seventh1 3 5 b7E G# B D

So a V7b9b13 in minor is nothing more than "the dominant chord with the rest of the harmonic minor scale piled on top". Playing the b9 and b13 in your lines or voicings announces a minor resolution before the i chord arrives. Line clichés

A line cliché is a chromatic line moving inside a chord that otherwise stays put. The most common one in minor runs from the root down through the major 7th and the minor 7th to the 6th: i – i(maj7) – i7 – i6. The chord symbols change every bar but the harmony is really one tonic chord with a moving inner (or top) voice. You will see it written out as four chords on charts, and you should recognise it instantly as one sound.

The descending line cliché on a minor tonic
The top note falls a half step per bar: root, major 7th, minor 7th, 6th. Keep the lower four notes still and let everything ring.

Two things to practise with it. First, play it and then continue to the iiø7 and V7b9 of the key so you hear the cliché as a way of filling four bars of i before the cadence; many minor-key tunes open exactly like this. Second, put the moving line in the bass instead of on top (root, major 7th, minor 7th, 6th on the 5th and 6th strings under a static minor shape); you will meet that version in walking-bass chord melody in Module 13.

Work the cycle of fourths with this exactly as you did in lesson 3. The chain is slightly less automatic than in major because the i chord does not become the V of the next key; think of each new tonic as a perfect 4th above the last and build iiø7 and V7 from it.

Exercises

  1. Three tables, one key. Pick a minor key and write out the diatonic sevenths of its natural, harmonic and melodic minor scales from memory. Check them against the engine tables (use the chord dictionary or transpose the A minor tables). Do a new key each day.
  2. Guide tones. Play the two-note guide-tone pairs through the minor ii–V–i in A, D, G, C and E minor, on strings 3–2 and again on strings 4–3. Say "moves" or "stays" for each voice.
  3. Arpeggios with the b9. Play the A minor arpeggio example, then transpose it to D minor (up five frets) and E minor (up seven frets, or down five). Then play it with the b13 instead of the b9 on the dominant.
  4. Cliché to cadence. Play the line cliché for four bars, then iiø7 for a bar, V7b9 for a bar, and i6 for two bars. Repeat in D minor and G minor using the shapes from the chords block moved to the new roots.
  5. Listen. Find three minor-key standards on the repertoire list and identify the minor ii–V–i in each, using the numerals above. Note whether the tonic is written as m6, mMaj7 or m7.
Practice

Daily, 25 minutes, metronome at 60–84 bpm.

  • Week 1: guide tones and drop-2 shapes through the minor ii–V–i in A, D, G, C and F minor, two bars per chord. Arpeggios in one position in the same keys, including the b9 on the dominant. Line cliché in A and D minor, then straight into the cadence.
  • Week 2: the full cycle of fourths through all twelve minor keys with guide tones, then with shapes, then with arpeggios (one bar per chord). Alternate the tonic between m6 and mMaj7 on each pass.
  • Every day: five minutes of ear training. Play a V7b9 chord and sing the b9 resolving down to the 5th of the i chord; play iiø7 and sing its b5.

Target: play the minor ii–V–i in any key within five seconds, name the source scale of any chord in a minor-key tune, and spot a line cliché on a chart at sight.
